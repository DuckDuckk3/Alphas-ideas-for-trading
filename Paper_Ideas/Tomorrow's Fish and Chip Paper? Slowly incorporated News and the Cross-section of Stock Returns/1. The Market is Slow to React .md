# Idea 1: The Market is Slow to React
## Page 9 - Para 1
The finding in Wang et al. (2018) suggests that previous returns did not capture news
signals in a one-month window. For the data studied in this paper, the correlation between
contemporaneous monthly returns and the news sentiment score is also very low (0.066)
on a monthly basis. It is perhaps surprising but consistent with Wang et al. (2018) that
initial market responses to the tone of news articles are insufficiently large. Otherwise, we
should observe that the market responds favourably to the news tone (i.e., a highly positive
correlation between the two sorting variables). Collectively, we use a sequential double-sorted
approach based on monthly stock returns and news sentiment scores to measure how quickly
information is incorporated. If returns cannot match news sentiment, it can be accepted
that stock prices did not catch the news signal promptly and a SI news item is defined.

## Explanation (In simple terms)  
Normally, you would expect a stock's price to instantly jump on good news and drop on bad news. However, this text highlights a surprising reality: the stock market is often slow to react. Over a one-month period, the actual change in a stock's price has a surprisingly weak connection to the positive or negative tone of its news. Because investors do not instantly or fully price in the news, researchers can measure this delay. If a piece of news is clearly positive (or negative) but the stock price barely moves, researchers flag it as an "SI news item"—meaning the market failed to catch the signal promptly.

## Core Concepts
- News Sentiment Score: A quantitative measure of how positive, negative, or neutral the tone of news articles is regarding a specific stock.
- Contemporaneous Returns: The actual stock price performance (gains or losses) happening during the exact same time frame the news is published.
- Market Underreaction: The tendency of investors to initially respond too weakly to new information (meaning the initial price jump/drop is "insufficiently large").
- Sequential Double-Sorted Approach: A research methodology where data is grouped by one variable, and then those groups are sub-divided by a second variable (e.g., grouping stocks first by their monthly returns, and then sorting those groups by their news sentiment scores) to spot patterns.
- SI News Item: A classification for a piece of news where the stock price failed to promptly reflect the news's sentiment (i.e., the stock return and news tone do not match).

## Ideas
1. The Myth of Instant Market Efficiency
Despite the assumption that stock prices instantly reflect all available public information, the data shows otherwise. Within a one-month window, the market systematically underreacts to the tone of media articles.
2. The Low Correlation Anomaly
The statistical proof of this underreaction is the extremely low correlation (just 0.066) between how positive the news is and how well the stock performs in that same month. If the market digested information perfectly, this correlation would be highly positive (good news = high returns immediately).
3. Exploiting the "SI" Gap
The discrepancy between news tone and price movement gives researchers (and traders) a way to measure the speed of information processing. By isolating "SI news items" (where the price lags behind the news signal), one can potentially predict future price movements as the market eventually "wakes up" and corrects the price to match the news.
