Backtesting: Key Pillars & Pitfalls
- `The danger of bad data:` If you ignore common backtesting errors, the results might be useless or even lead to massive financial losses.  
- `Statistical proof:` High return is meaningless without proof. Use Hypothesis Testing and Monte Carlo simulations to ensure your success isn't just "luck".  
- `Trade volume:` The higher trades, the higher statistical significance.
- `Market shifts:` nothing ensures a perfect backtest is always winning. Regime shifts can make past success irrelevant to the future.
- `The right tool:` Choose a platform that handles both backtesting and live execution.

## The Importance of Backtesting
Backtesting is feeding historical data to see how your trading strat would have performed. However, it isn't just about checking if a strat actually "works", it's about translating a theoretical idea into a precise, executable reality.  
`"Devil is in the Details"`  
Published articles often skip small stuff to focus on macro ideas. However, trading success usually depends on micro-decision:
- `Timing`: Do we need to buy exactly at the open, or 5 minutes later?
- `Price`: Do you trigger a trade, based on the "Last Price", the "Bid" or the "Ask".
- `Synchronization`: If trading in two different markets, do their closing bells simultaneously ring at the same time?  
=> `The solution`: Backtesting forces you to pin down these details so your automated execution matches your plan perfectly.

`Spotting "Invisible" Pitfalls`
Backtesting puts the strategy under a microscope to find reality-checks that authors might miss:
- `Liquidity`: Can you actually "short" that stock, or is it impossible to borrow?
- `Performance Inflation`: Most errors in backtesting make a strategy look better than it is. Doing it yourself helps you strip away this false optimism.

`The "True" Out-of-Sample Test`  
Authors often "tweak" their models until they fit their data (even their "out-of-sample" data).  
Once a strategy is published, the clock starts.  
By backtesting the period after the paper was written, you are doing the only "honest" test possible to see if the strategy actually works in the real world. 

`Constant Refinement (The Scientific Method)`  
By backtesting a strat ourselves => we can often find ways to refine and improve the strat to make it more profitable or less risky.  
This process should follow a *"scientific method"*:
- `Hypothesis`: based on our own intuition about the market or from some published research
- `Backtest`: confirm or refute the hypothesis. If the results of the backtest aren't good enough, modify the *hypothesis* and repeat the progress.

`Key Takeaway:` If you didn't build the backtest yourself, you don't truly own the strategy. `Performance` of a strategy is often very sensitive
to details, and small changes in these details can bring about substantial improvements. These changes can be changing the look-back time period for determining the MA, or entering orders at the open instead of close.  
=> Backtesting a strategy allows us to experiment with every detail.

## Common Pitfalls of Backtesting
Although almost every strategy allows for unique opportunities in committing errors in backtesting, there are a number of common themes, some generally appicable to all markets, others pertain to specific ones.  

### Look-ahead Bias  
Backtest program using tomorrow's prices to determine today's trading signals.  
A common example of look-ahead bias is to use a day’s high or low price to
determine the entry signal during the same day during backtesting.

=> Look-ahead bias is essentially a `programming error` and can infect
only a backtest program but not a live trading program (there is no way a live trading program can obtain future information).  
This difference points to an obvious way to `avoid look-ahead bias`:
- If your backtesting and live trading programs are the same, and the only difference is what kind of data you are feeding into the program (historical
data in the former, and live market data in the latter), then there can be *no
look-ahead bias* in the program.
