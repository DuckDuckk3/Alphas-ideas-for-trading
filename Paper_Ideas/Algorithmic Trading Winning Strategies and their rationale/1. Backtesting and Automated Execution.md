# CHAPTER 1: Backtesting and Automated Execution
Backtesting: Key Pillars & Pitfalls
- `The danger of bad data:` If you ignore common backtesting errors, the results might be useless or even lead to massive financial losses.  
- `Statistical proof:` High return is meaningless without proof. Use Hypothesis Testing and Monte Carlo simulations to ensure your success isn't just "luck".  
- `Trade volume:` The higher trades, the higher statistical significance.
- `Market shifts:` nothing ensures a perfect backtest is always winning. Regime shifts can make past success irrelevant to the future.
- `The right tool:` Choose a platform that handles both backtesting and live execution.

## The Importance of Backtesting
Backtesting is feeding historical data to see how your trading strat would have performed. However, it isn't just about checking if a strat actually "works", it's about translating a theoretical idea into a precise, executable reality.  
`"Devil is in the Details"`  
Published articles often skip small stuff to focus on macro ideas. However, trading success usually depends on micro-decision:
- `Timing`: Do we need to buy exactly at the open, or 5 minutes later?
- `Price`: Do you trigger a trade, based on the "Last Price", the "Bid", or the "Ask"?
- `Synchronization`: If trading in two different markets, do their closing bells simultaneously ring at the same time?  
=> `The solution`: Backtesting forces you to pin down these details so your automated execution matches your plan perfectly.

`Spotting "Invisible" Pitfalls`
Backtesting puts the strategy under a microscope to find reality checks that authors might miss:
- `Liquidity`: Can you actually "short" that stock, or is it impossible to borrow?
- `Performance Inflation`: Most errors in backtesting make a strategy look better than it is. Doing it yourself helps you strip away this false optimism.

`The "True" Out-of-Sample Test`  
Authors often "tweak" their models until they fit their data (even their "out-of-sample" data).  
Once a strategy is published, the clock starts.  
By backtesting the period after the paper was written, you are doing the only "honest" test possible to see if the strategy actually works in the real world. 

`Constant Refinement (The Scientific Method)`  
By backtesting a strat ourselves => we can often find ways to refine and improve the strat to make it more profitable or less risky.  
This process should follow a *"scientific method"*:
- `Hypothesis`: based on our own intuition about the market or from some published research
- `Backtest`: confirm or refute the hypothesis. If the results of the backtest aren't good enough, modify the *hypothesis* and repeat the process.

`Key Takeaway:` If you didn't build the backtest yourself, you don't truly own the strategy. `Performance` of a strategy is often very sensitive
to details, and small changes in these details can bring about substantial improvements. These changes can be changing the look-back time period for determining the MA, or entering orders at the open instead of close.  
=> Backtesting a strategy allows us to experiment with every detail.

## Common Pitfalls of Backtesting
Although almost every strategy allows for unique opportunities in committing errors in backtesting, there are several common themes: some are generally applicable to all markets, others pertain to specific ones.  

### Look-ahead Bias  
Backtest program using tomorrow's prices to determine today's trading signals.  
A common example of look-ahead bias is to use a day’s high or low price to
determine the entry signal during the same day during backtesting.

=> `Look-ahead bias` is essentially a `programming error` and can infect only a backtest program, but not a live trading program (there is no way a live trading program can obtain future information).  
This difference points to an obvious way to `avoid look-ahead bias`:
- If your `backtesting and live trading programs are the same`, and the only difference is what kind of data you are feeding into the program (historical
data in the former, and live market data in the latter), then there can be *no
look-ahead bias* in the program.

### Data-Snooping Bias and the Beauty of Linearity
Data-snooping bias is a term used to describe the tendency to overfit to random past market patterns, making historical performance look better than it actually is.  
=> These patterns are unlikely to recur in the future.  
=> Fitted model is unlikely to have much predictive power.  

#### `Way to detect data-snooping bias`: 
- Test the model on out-of-sample data and reject a model that doesn't pass this test.

- BUT instead of removing the model entirely, most of us will tweak the model so it performs well on even the out-of-sample data. By doing this, we have just turned the out-of-sample data into in-sample data.

=> Then you are unwilling to toss out a model, or if you have a small data set
and really need to tweak the model using most of this data, you should consider the idea of `cross-validation`.  
==> `Cross-validation`: Select some different subsets of data for training and tweaking the model, making sure the model performs well on these subsets.  

We prefer models with a `high Sharpe ratio` and `short maximum drawdown duration` as this almost automatically ensures that the model will pass the cross-validation test: the only subsets where the model will fail the test are those rare drawdown periods.

#### `Strategy Construction: Power of Simplicity`   
This guide explores how to minimize data-snooping bias by favoring linear models and simple parameter sets over complex, nonlinear alternatives.  

1. `The Core Principle: Simplicity vs. Bias`  
  - `Golden Rule`: Make the model as simple as possible with the fewest parameters.  
  - `The Trap`: A model with few parameters but many "complicated trading rules" is still highly susceptible to data-snooping bias.  
  - `Linear > Nonlinear`: Nonlinear models fit historical data better but rarely predict future values better. Most nonlinear models can be approximated by a simpler Taylor-series expansion (linear approximation).

2. `Probability & Occam’s Razor`:  
  - `The Gaussian Default`: While markets have "fat tails," choosing alternative distributions (Student’s t, Pareto, etc.) introduces arbitrariness.
  - `Occam’s Razor`: Unless there is a massive theoretical reason otherwise, assume a Gaussian (Normal) distribution to avoid over-fitting to a finite set of past observations.

3. `Linear Trading & "Scaling-In"`
  - Linearity applies not just to price prediction, but also to how you allocate capital.
  - `The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Formula`:  
    $dy(t) = (\lambda y(t - 1) + \mu)dt + d\epsilon$  
This model's mean reversion, where the price change `(dy)` is proportional to the distance from the mean.  
=> `Execution`: Instead of a binary "Buy/Sell" at a threshold (like Bollinger Bands), a linear approach suggests "Scaling-in": your position size should be proportional to the deviation from the mean.  

4. `Predictive Models`: The Equal-Weight Advantage  
Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman noted that `formulas` assigning `equal weights` to all predictors are often superior because they aren't affected by `"accidents of sampling"`.  
- `A`. The Normalized Factor Model: To predict tomorrow's return ($R$) using multiple factors ($f$):
    - `Normalize` factors into `Z-scores`:
    $$z(i) = \frac{f(i) - \text{mean}(f)}{\text{std}(f)}$$
    - `Combine for Prediction`:
    $$R = \text{mean}(R) + \text{std}(R) \cdot \left( \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{sign}(i) \cdot z(i) \right) / n$$  
    Where $\text{sign}(i)$ is the direction of the historical correlation between factor $i$ and returns.  
- `B`. The Simplified Ranking Method: If your goal is simply to rank stocks (for a Long-Short portfolio), use `Signed Ranks`:  
  $$\text{rank}_{s} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \text{sign}(i) \cdot \text{rank}_{s}(i)$$  
  Example: `Joel Greenblatt’s "Magic Formula"` used only `two factors` (Return on Capital and Earnings Yield) to rank stocks, significantly outperforming the `S&P500` from `1988–2004`.  

5. `The Final Reality Check`
No matter how simple your model is, bias will creep in.  
You must perform:
  - `Walk-forward Testing`: A true out-of-sample test.
  - `Paper Trading`: Testing the logic in real-time.
  - `Incubation` (Small Money): Trade with minimal real leverage to catch execution issues that paper trading misses.
  - `The Rule of Thumb`: Expect your live Sharpe Ratio to be roughly half of your backtest value.

## Stock Splits and Dividend Adjustments  
Adjusting for corporate actions is one of the most critical steps in data cleaning. Without these adjustments, your backtest will witness "phantom" price drops that look like a market crash but are actually just accounting changes.

1. `The Problem: "Phantom" Price Drops`
When a company undergoes a `Stock Split` or pays a `Dividend`, the share price drops instantly.
  - `Stock Split (N-to-1)`: The price is divided by N. If you don't adjust, your algos might see a 50% "crash" and trigger a massive (and incorrect) sell signal. The truth is that, if a stock splits 2-for-1, the price is cut in half. You own twice as many shares, so you haven't lost money.
  - `Dividends`: When a company pays a cash dividend of $d per share, the stock price typically drops by that same amount on the ex-dividend date.

2. `The Solution: Back-Adjustment`  
To keep your trading signals accurate, you must "smooth out" the history so the total market value remains consistent.
   - `For Splits`: You must divide all historical prices *before* the split date by N. (Conversely, for a reverse split, you multiply by N).
   - `For Dividends`: You subtract the dividend amount from all historical prices before the ex-date because that cash is leaving the company's books.
   - `The Risk`: A backtest looking only at raw prices will see a sudden, massive drop. This could trigger a "sell" signal for a "crash" that is actually just a neutral accounting change.  
  
3. `Live Trading vs. Backtesting`  
This actually isn't just looking at the past. On the morning of an `Ex-Date` (the day the adjustment takes effect), your live trading model must update its entire historical database. If your model uses a 20-day MA, that average will be wrong on the ex-date unless the previous 19 days of data are adjusted to match today's new price level.

### Resource Guide for Adjustments:
Manually adjusting data is tedious. Use these tools to simplify the process:
  - `Earnings.com`: Great for tracking upcoming (future) splits and dividends so your software can prepare in advance.  
  - `CSIData.com`: A reliable source for historical data that is already adjusted, saving you the math.
