### Explanation (in simple terms)
While the strategy works on both sides, the "bad news" lasts much longer than the "good news." If an industry reacts poorly to a merger, that negativity can haunt those stocks for up to a whole year. On the other hand, the "boost" from good news is a short sprint that mostly disappears after the first month. The text also suggests that this happens because it is harder for people to sell stocks they don't own (short-selling), which prevents the market from fixing "bad" prices as quickly as "good" ones.  

### Key concepts
- Horizon Diminishment: The slow decline in profit (from 105 bps down to 24-30 bps) as you hold the trade longer.

- The "Short Leg" Advantage: The discovery that industries with negative reactions underperform more reliably and for a longer duration (up to 12 months).

- Short-Selling Constraints: A market barrier that stops investors from betting against stocks, causing the market to "underreact" to bad news.

- Symmetry vs. Asymmetry: Raw returns show equal profit from both sides, but once you adjust for risk (Size/BM), the short side is the only one that remains statistically "real" over the long term.

### Ideas
Prioritize the Shorts: If you have limited capital, focus your Alpha on shorting the industries with negative M&A reactions; it’s a more "robust" and "significant" signal.

Asymmetric Holding Periods: You could design a strategy that holds "Long" positions for only 1 month (fast exit) but holds "Short" positions for 3–6 months (slow exit).

Identifying Market Friction: Use this Alpha as a tool to find "mispriced" industries where short-selling constraints are preventing the price from dropping to its true value.

Benchmark Comparison: Compare your portfolio against a "No M&A" benchmark. The goal is to consistently stay 0.5% above it for longs and 0.5% below it for shorts at the one-month mark.

### Key takeaway
The strategy's true power lies in the "short leg," where industry underperformance following negative merger news persists for up to a year, likely due to market barriers that slow down the correction of bad news.
